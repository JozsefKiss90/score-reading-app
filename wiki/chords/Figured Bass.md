# Figured Bass

## Definition

Figured bass names a chord's inversion by the intervals its upper tones make **above the bass note**: 5/3 for root position, 6 (short for 6/3) for first inversion, 6/4 for second. The drills attach the figure to the [[Roman Numeral]] — ii6, I6/4 — and treat it as an instruction about which note must be lowest.

## Why it matters

Figures are how this app (and three centuries of notation before it) writes [[Chord Inversion]] compactly. More importantly, in the drills a figure is *graded*: ii6 is not a caption but a demand — play ii with its third in the bass, or fail. Reading 6 and 6/4 fluently is also the entry ticket to later material, where the dominant seventh's figures and the cadential 6/4 assume this page.

## Prerequisites

[[Chord Inversion]] and its three positions, the [[Interval]] numbers being counted, and the [[Roman Numeral]] the figure attaches to.

## Explanation

**Count upward from the bass.** Whatever note is lowest, name the intervals the other chord tones form above it:

| Figure | Bass note | Position | V in C major |
|--------|-----------|----------|--------------|
| 5/3 (usually omitted) | root | root position | G–B–D, G lowest |
| 6 (short for 6/3) | third | first inversion | B lowest; G now a sixth above |
| 6/4 | fifth | second inversion | D lowest; G a fourth, B a sixth above |

Root position stacks a third and a fifth over the bass — 5/3 — so ordinary numerals (V, ii) silently mean root position. In first inversion the root has moved above the bass and sits a sixth over it: 6/3, conventionally shortened to just **6**. In second inversion the root is a fourth above the bass: **6/4**. These three figures — 5/3, 6, 6/4 — are exactly the vocabulary the triad drills print.

**The figure is a performance instruction.** In C major, ii6 means D–F–A with **F** as the lowest sounding note. The drill only passes when it is; the other tones may sit in any octave. The figure never renames the chord — ii6 is still ii, still D minor — it constrains how the chord meets the floor.

**Why the cadence loves 6.** First inversion earns its keep in the approach to a close: ii6 puts degree 4̂ in the bass, so the bass line walks 4̂–5̂–1̂ (in C: F–G–C) — the smoothest possible run into the final chord. This is the figure at work rather than on display.

**Beyond triads.** A four-note chord makes four bass choices and its own figures — 7, 6/5, 4/3, 4/2 — introduced with the [[Dominant Seventh Chord]]. And one figure is famous enough to have a page of its own: the [[Cadential Six-Four]], where a 6/4 shape over the dominant's bass turns out not to be the chord its spelling claims.

## In the Lab

Every drill in the *Inversions* category captions its measures with these figures, so they are met first as labels. They become tasks at *ii6 at the cadence*, where the 6 is assessed (F must be the bass in C major), and at *Bass-line dictation*, where a progression sounds and you answer with the bass line the figures predict — reading turned into hearing.

## Common confusions

- **6/4 the chord vs. 6/4 the meter.** Identical digits, unrelated meanings: after a numeral it is a second-inversion chord; after a clef it is a [[Time Signature]]. Both live on the same page of the score, so the clash is real.
- **"6" is not a sixth chord.** It adds no sixth to the harmony; it is shorthand for 6/3, a plain first-inversion triad. Nothing is added — something is re-bottomed.
- **Figures count from the bass, not the root.** The 6 in ii6 measures from F (the bass), not from D (the root). Counting from the root makes every figure come out wrong.
- **A figure is not a new chord.** ii and ii6 have identical notes and the same job in the key; the figure only pins the bass.
