# Chord Inversion

## Definition

An inversion keeps a chord's tones but puts a different one in the bass: **root position** has the root lowest, **first inversion** the third, **second inversion** the fifth. The chord's identity — its notes and its name — does not change; only the bass does.

## Why it matters

Real music rarely keeps every chord sitting on its root: composers invert chords to make the bass line walk in steps instead of leaps. Reading music therefore demands the skill this concept trains — recognising the *same* chord under a moving bass. It is also the app's first case where what you play is graded positionally: a block inversion drill passes only when the demanded note is the lowest one sounding.

## Prerequisites

The [[Triad]] and its named members (root, third, fifth), and [[Interval Inversion]] — the two-note version of the same flip.

## Explanation

**Three positions of one chord.** C major is C–E–G. Move the C up an octave: E–G–C, first inversion. Move the E up too: G–C–E, second inversion. The same three [[Pitch Class]]es each time; the bass climbs root → third → fifth.

**Two ways to write it**, both used by the drills:

- **Slash chords** name the bass after a slash: C, C/E, C/G. Everything before the slash is the chord; after it, the required bass note.
- **Figures** count intervals above the bass: 5/3 for root position, 6 for first inversion, 6/4 for second. [[Figured Bass]] owns the counting; here it is enough that each position has its number.

**What changes and what doesn't.** The chord tones are invariant — a C major chord in any inversion answers to "C major", contains the same three pitch classes, keeps its [[Triad Quality]], and keeps its [[Roman Numeral]]. What changes is the bass and, with it, the colour: root position sounds grounded, first inversion lighter and more mobile, second inversion unstable, leaning to resolve. That instability is why the second inversion gets special handling at cadences — the [[Cadential Six-Four]], the one famous case where the bass position changes what the chord *does*. Everywhere else, [[Harmonic Function]] survives inversion.

**Doubling and spacing.** Above the bass, the upper tones may sit in any octave and any order, and a tone may be doubled — the same pitch class sounding in two octaves at once. None of that changes the chord or its inversion. Two questions settle everything: *which pitch classes sound* (the chord) and *which one is lowest* (the inversion). Spacing — how far apart the voices sit — is a matter of voicing and taste, taken up properly with voice leading later.

Then *Major inversions* inverts the core degrees (I, ii, IV, V) across all twelve major keys and *Natural minor inversions* does i, iv, v and VII in minor; *ii6 at the cadence* puts first inversion to musical work; *Bass-line dictation* turns the reading skill into a hearing one. Grading is honest about the point: a block-inversion drill passes only when the demanded chord member is the lowest sounding note.

## Common confusions

- **C/E is not a new chord.** It is C major with E in the bass. The slash adds information; it doesn't change identity.
- **The lowest note is the root only in root position.** Assuming bass = root is the single most common inversion error, and exactly what the drills are built to break.
- **Chord inversion ≠ interval inversion.** Interval inversion flips two notes around an octave; chord inversion re-bottoms a three-note stack. Related ideas, different objects — the intervals page owns the two-note case.
- **Inversion ≠ re-voicing.** Shuffling the *upper* tones between octaves changes the spacing, not the inversion. Only the bass note defines the position.
