# Diatonic Triads

## Definition

The diatonic triads of a key are the seven chords made by building a triad on each scale degree using only the scale's own notes — the key's complete native chord set. Every key has exactly seven, and their qualities fall in a fixed, key-independent pattern.

## Why it matters

This set is the raw material of nearly every drill in the app: full-key drills play all seven, degree drills transpose one of them through twelve keys, cadence and function drills combine them into progressions. Because the quality pattern is identical in every major key, learning C major's seven chords means learning all twelve keys' chords — only the spellings change.

## Prerequisites

The [[Major Scale]] that supplies the notes, the [[Scale Degree]] positions the chords stand on, the [[Triad]] itself, and [[Triad Quality]] for naming what comes out.

## Explanation

**Construction.** On each degree of the scale, take that note, skip one, take the next, skip one, take the next — three alternate scale notes, stacked in thirds. No accidentals, no borrowed tones: the scale does all the deciding.

**The seven chords of C major:**

| Degree | Numeral | Notes | Quality |
|--------|---------|-------|---------|
| 1̂ | I | C–E–G | major |
| 2̂ | ii | D–F–A | minor |
| 3̂ | iii | E–G–B | minor |
| 4̂ | IV | F–A–C | major |
| 5̂ | V | G–B–D | major |
| 6̂ | vi | A–C–E | minor |
| 7̂ | vii° | B–D–F | diminished |

**The pattern is fixed.** Major on 1, 4, 5; minor on 2, 3, 6; diminished on 7 — in G major, in E♭ major, in every major key. The reason: the scale's two half steps sit at fixed positions, so the third-sizes each degree can reach are fixed too. The pattern is a property of the *scale shape*, not of any particular key. The labels in the table's numeral column belong to [[Roman Numeral]] notation, which exists precisely to name this key-independent pattern.

**Minor keys have their own set.** The [[Natural Minor Scale]] yields i, ii°, III, iv, v, VI, VII — in A minor: Am, B°, C, Dm, Em, F, G. Same construction rule, different quality layout, because the step pattern underneath moved. (These are the same seven chords as C major renumbered — the relative relationship at work.)

**One chord, many jobs.** C–E–G is I in C major, IV in G major, and V in F major. The chord is one object; its diatonic *role* depends on which key claims it.

## Common confusions

- **vii° looks major, isn't.** Students autocomplete "triad on B = B major". Diatonic B in C major is B–D–F — diminished. The drills' classic mislabel.
- **Diatonic ≠ frequently used.** iii is fully diatonic and comparatively rare; the [[Applied Dominant]] chords met much later are common and *not* diatonic. Diatonic is a fact about scale membership, not popularity.
- **The pattern belongs to the mode.** Major's M-m-m-M-M-m-° and natural minor's m-°-M-m-m-M-M are different patterns, not variations of one — swap the scale and the whole chord set re-colours.
- **Seven chords, not seven sounds.** Between keys the numerals repeat but the pitches don't; within a key the pitches overlap heavily (every scale note sits in three different triads). Neither the chords nor the notes are one-to-one.
