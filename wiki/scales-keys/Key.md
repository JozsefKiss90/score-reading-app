# Key

## Definition

A key is music organised around one home note — the **tonic** — and the scale built from it. A key is named by its tonic plus its mode: *C major* means "home is C, using the major scale's notes"; *A minor* means "home is A, using the minor scale's".

## Why it matters

Everything in the app happens *in a key*: every drill announces one ("in G major"), every chord is judged against one, and every [[Transposition]] is a change of one. The key is what makes seven of the twelve notes *expected* and the rest *outsiders* — the frame that gives "wrong note" its meaning.

## Prerequisites

The [[Note Names]], the [[Major Scale]] as the pattern a key instantiates, and the [[Octave]] fold that lets one letter stand for a note in every register.

## Explanation

**Home and gravity.** Play the C major scale and stop on B — it feels unfinished; land on C and it settles. That pull toward one note is the experience a key names. The tonic is not the loudest or most frequent note; it is the note everything else is heard *in relation to*.

**A key = a tonic + a mode.** Choose a home note, then choose which scale pattern to lay on it. Twelve possible tonics × two drilled modes (major, and minor via the [[Natural Minor Scale]]) gives the twenty-four keys the app works in. Each key's scale fixes its seven member notes; those members and the chords they build are called **diatonic** to the key, and everything else needs an [[Accidental]].

**Key vs. scale.** The scale is the inventory — seven notes listed in order. The key is the *allegiance*: a whole piece, with melodies and chords in any order, can be "in C major" without ever playing the scale as a run. You practise scales; you make music in keys.

**Keys are written down as signatures.** Because a key fixes its accidentals up front, notation declares them once after the clef — the [[Key Signature]]. One signature serves two keys (a major and its relative minor), which is why signature alone never fully identifies the key — see [[Relative and Parallel Keys]].

**Keys relate by distance.** Two keys sharing most of their notes feel close; the [[Circle of Fifths]] is the map of exactly that. Moving a piece wholesale from one key to another is transposition.

## In the Lab

Every drill runs in a declared key, and the *Scales* category makes the key itself the unit of practice: its full-key drills walk all seven diatonic triads of one key at a time, so you experience the key as a complete, self-consistent set rather than isolated chords.

## Common confusions

- **Key vs. key signature.** The signature is the *notation* for a key's accidentals; the key is the musical gravity. C major and A minor share a signature but are different keys — different tonics, different pull.
- **Key vs. piano key.** English overloads the word: the drills say "key of G" (a tonal home) and "press the key" (a physical lever). Context always disambiguates, but the collision trips up beginners.
- **Key vs. scale.** The scale is a list; the key is a frame of reference. Music in C major rarely runs C-D-E-F-G-A-B, yet every note is heard against that set and its tonic.
