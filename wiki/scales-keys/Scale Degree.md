# Scale Degree

## Definition

A scale degree is a note's numbered position inside its scale, counted from the home note: in C major, C is degree 1, D is degree 2, and so on to B at degree 7. Each degree also has a traditional name — tonic, supertonic, mediant, subdominant, dominant, submediant, leading tone.

## Why it matters

Degrees are the app's real vocabulary. "The chord on E" is a fact about one key; "the chord on degree 3" is a fact about *every* key. The whole curriculum — degree drills, chord labels, functions, cadences — speaks in degrees precisely so that what you learn in C major transfers to the other eleven keys unchanged.

## Prerequisites

The [[Major Scale]] (degrees are positions in it) and the [[Key]] whose tonic the counting starts from.

## Explanation

**Numbers.** Degrees are written 1̂ through 7̂ (the caret distinguishes a degree from a plain number). Degree 8 is just degree 1 an [[Octave]] up — the numbering folds like the note names do.

**Names.** Each degree has a name that describes its *relationship to the tonic*, not its letter:

| Degree | Name | In C major | Why it's called that |
|--------|------|------------|----------------------|
| 1̂ | Tonic | C | The tone: home itself |
| 2̂ | Supertonic | D | Directly *above* the tonic |
| 3̂ | Mediant | E | *Midway* from tonic up to dominant |
| 4̂ | Subdominant | F | A fifth *below* the tonic (the under-dominant) |
| 5̂ | Dominant | G | A [[Perfect Fifth]] *above* the tonic; the strongest pole after home |
| 6̂ | Submediant | A | Midway from tonic *down* to subdominant |
| 7̂ | Leading tone | B | A [[Semitone]] below the tonic, leading home |

The names are symmetric around the tonic: dominant a fifth up, subdominant a fifth down; mediant midway up, submediant midway down. The [[Leading Tone]] is the odd one out — named for what it *does*, pulling up into the tonic.

**In minor.** The [[Natural Minor Scale]] uses the same numbering, but its seventh degree sits a whole tone below the tonic, so it loses the pull and the name: it is called the **subtonic** instead. (Restoring a true leading tone in minor is the harmonic-minor story, told in the minor-modes pages.)

**Degrees carry chords.** Build a [[Triad]] on each degree and you get the key's seven diatonic chords. The drills label these with Roman numerals — I for the tonic chord, V for the dominant chord, and so on — uppercase and lowercase encoding the quality (the chord pages cover that convention in full). The numeral is just the degree number wearing chord clothes.

## In the Lab

The *Degrees & Transposition* category is built directly on this page's idea: each drill fixes **one** degree and transposes its triad through all twelve keys, block or arpeggiated — the *Major degree transposition* lesson covers I through vii°, the *Minor degree transposition* lesson i through VII. Spelling and pitches change every round; the degree, its quality and its role stay put. That invariance is the point.

## Common confusions

- **Degree number vs. interval number.** Degree 5 is a position in the scale; a fifth is a distance between two notes. They coincide only when measuring from the tonic — the [[Interval]] page owns the counting rules.
- **"Dominant" the degree vs. dominant the chord-family.** Here *dominant* names degree 5̂. The harmony pages later use *dominant* for a whole family of chords that share its function; same word, widening meaning. Learn it here as a position first.
- **Degrees are key-relative.** G is the dominant *of C major* and the tonic *of G major*. A degree name is meaningless without saying which key you are counting in.
- **7̂ has two names.** A semitone below tonic: leading tone. A whole tone below (natural minor): subtonic. The drills' minor keys build their VII chord on the subtonic, which surprises students expecting a leading tone — the natural-minor page tells that story.
