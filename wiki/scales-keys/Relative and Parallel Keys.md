# Relative and Parallel Keys

## Definition

Two keys are **relative** when they share every note but have different homes: C major and A minor use the same seven white keys. Two keys are **parallel** when they share the same home but different notes: C major and C minor both centre on C. Every major key has exactly one of each.

## Why it matters

These are the two ways a major and a minor key can be siblings, and the drills use both: relatives explain why one key signature serves two keys (and why the circle of fifths has an inner ring), while the parallel comparison is the clean way to *hear* what minor changes — same tonic, three notes lowered. Mixing the two up is probably the single most common key-relationship error, in and out of the app.

## Prerequisites

The [[Key]] as tonic plus mode, the [[Major Scale]] and [[Natural Minor Scale]], and the [[Key Signature]] the relative pair shares.

## Explanation

**Relative: same notes, different home.** Take C major and start its notes from degree 6̂ instead of 1̂: A B C D E F G — the natural minor scale on A. Nothing about the pitch inventory changed; only the gravity moved. Because the notes are identical, the [[Key Signature]] is identical too: every signature names one major key *and* its relative minor. The rule in both directions: the relative minor sits on the major key's 6̂ (C major → A minor); the relative major sits on the minor key's 3̂ (A minor → C major) — a minor third apart, always.

**Parallel: same home, different notes.** C major and C minor both call C home, but minor lowers degrees 3̂, 6̂ and 7̂ (E♭, A♭, B♭). Signatures now differ — by exactly three accidentals in the flat direction, three counter-clockwise steps on the [[Circle of Fifths]]. The parallel comparison is what statements like "minor lowers the third" implicitly mean: you cannot lower a degree relative to a *relative* key (the notes are the same); you lower it relative to the *parallel* major.

**Which comparison when?** Reading signatures and navigating the circle → think in relatives. Explaining minor's colour, or why a piece "brightens" when it ends major → think in parallels. Same two words, two different questions.

## Common confusions

- **Relative vs. parallel — the classic.** *Relative* = related by **notes** (C major / A minor). *Parallel* = related by **tonic** (C major / C minor). If the letter name of the key changes, you moved to a relative; if the letter stays and the accidentals change, a parallel.
- **"Same signature = same key."** A signature names a relative *pair*; the music's resting note decides which of the two you are in. Drills in A minor look, on paper, exactly like drills in C major until you notice where they land.
- **The relative minor is not "the major, played sad".** It has a different tonic, different degree numbers, and a different diatonic chord set (i ii° III … not I ii iii …). Shared notes, separate identities.
