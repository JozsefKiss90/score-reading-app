# Key Signature

## Definition

A key signature is the group of sharps or flats printed just after the [[Clef]] at the start of every staff line. It applies its [[Accidental|accidentals]] to every note of the matching letter, in every octave, for the whole piece — a key's standing spelling, written once instead of marked on each note.

## Why it matters

Signatures are how notation says *which key you are in* before a single note sounds. Reading them turns a forest of would-be accidentals into one glance — and because the number of sharps or flats maps one-to-one onto the circle of fifths, the signature is also the fastest route to naming the key of any drill the app puts in front of you.

## Prerequisites

The [[Staff]] and [[Clef]] it sits on, the [[Accidental]] symbols it is made of, and the [[Major Scale]] whose forced sharps or flats it collects. Knowing what a [[Key]] is makes the "signature = key's spelling" idea land.

## Explanation

**Where the accidentals come from.** Spell G major and the pattern forces F♯; spell D major and it forces F♯ *and* C♯. A signature simply gathers a key's forced accidentals and declares them once. G major's signature is one sharp; D major's is two.

**The accidentals come in a fixed order.** Sharps always appear as F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯ E♯ B♯ — each new sharp a fifth above the last — and flats as B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ G♭ C♭ F♭, each a fifth below. A three-sharp signature is always F♯ C♯ G♯, never some other trio. That fixed order is the [[Circle of Fifths]] written into notation: one step clockwise from C adds the next sharp in the sequence.

**Reach.** A signature governs *every* octave of its letter, for the whole piece: one F♯ in the signature sharpens every F on the [[Grand Staff]]. An in-measure accidental can override it locally — a ♮ cancels the signature's sharp for that letter until the barline — then the signature resumes.

**One signature, two keys.** Every signature serves a major key *and* its relative minor: no sharps or flats is C major or A minor; one sharp is G major or E minor. The notes alone cannot say which — the music's home note decides. That pairing is the subject of [[Relative and Parallel Keys]].

## In the Lab

Every drill rendered outside C major or A minor carries its key signature on the grand staff, so signature-reading is exercised constantly. The *Circle of Fifths* category opens the interactive circle, where each key's position *is* its signature — the wheel doubles as a signature reference you can launch drills from.

## In the Atlas

The Circle of Fifths view arranges all the signatures in order: steps from the top count the sharps (clockwise) or flats (counter-clockwise).

## Common confusions

- **Signature vs. accidental.** Same symbols, different reach: a signature is global (this letter, every octave, whole piece), an accidental is local (this note's letter, this measure).
- **A signature names two keys, not one.** Three sharps means A major *or* F♯ minor. Students who memorise "three sharps = A major" get minor-key drills wrong; check where the music comes to rest.
- **The signature marks letters, not staff positions.** The F♯ printed on the top line sharpens *every* F, including ones on [[Ledger Line|ledger lines]] two octaves down.
