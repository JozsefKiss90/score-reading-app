# Third

## Definition

A third is the [[Interval]] spanning three letter names — from a note to the note two scale steps above it. It comes in two diatonic sizes: the **major third** (M3, four semitones) and the **minor third** (m3, three semitones), and it is the building block chords are stacked from.

## Why it matters

Western harmony is built in thirds. Every [[Triad]] is two thirds stacked; every seventh chord is three. The *only* thing separating a bright major chord from a dark minor one is which size of third sits on the bottom — so hearing and spelling the M3/m3 difference is the single most leveraged interval skill in the app.

## Prerequisites

The [[Interval]] page's two-layer naming (number from letters, quality from semitones), and the [[Semitone]].

## Explanation

Count letters first: C up to E touches C, D, E — three letters, so it's a third. On the [[Staff]] a third always sits line-to-adjacent-line or space-to-adjacent-space, which makes it the easiest interval to spot at sight.

Now count semitones to get the quality:

- **C–E**: C→C♯→D→D♯→E is **four semitones** — a *major third*. Bright, open.
- **E–G**: E→F→F♯→G is **three semitones** — a *minor third*. Darker, softer.

Same letter distance, different size — and your ear can learn the difference faster than your counting: the major third is the start of a major chord, the minor third the start of a minor one.

**The two thirds inside a triad.** Stack a third on top of a third and you have a triad. In C major, C–E–G is an M3 (C–E) with an m3 (E–G) on top; A–C–E flips the order, m3 then M3. That ordering *is* the triad's quality — the full four-quality catalogue lives on the [[Triad]] page; what this page adds is that the *only* raw material in it is the two sizes of third.

Walk up the C-major scale building a third on every note and the sizes alternate irregularly — that irregularity is exactly why the seven diatonic triads don't all share one quality.

## Common confusions

- **"Third" ≠ "three semitones".** The *number* three comes from letters; the semitone count is three *or* four depending on quality. A three-semitone interval spelled C–D♯ isn't a third at all — it's an augmented second.
- **Bigger-sounding isn't "major".** From E, the white-key third E–G is minor even though no accidental appears; whether a third is major or minor depends on where the semitones E–F and B–C fall inside it, not on black keys.

## Related Concepts
