# Interval

## Definition

An interval is the distance between two notes, named in two layers: a **number** from counting letter names (a third, a fifth) and a **quality** from the exact size in semitones (major, minor, perfect). Two intervals can share a number and still differ in quality — the quality is the finer of the two measurements, and the one your ear notices first.

## Why it matters

Intervals are the units chords are built from. The app's whole harmony ladder — triad qualities, seventh chords, why a dominant chord pulls home — reduces to which intervals are stacked in what order. Learn to name an interval's number *and* quality and you can take any chord apart instead of memorising it whole.

## Prerequisites

The [[Note Names]], the [[Semitone]] as the unit of measurement, the [[Whole Tone]], and the [[Octave]] as the distance where names repeat.

## Explanation

**The number: count letters, inclusively.** From C up to E, count the letters you touch: C, D, E — three letters, so C–E is a *third*. C up to G touches C, D, E, F, G — five letters, a *fifth*. Count both endpoints; never count keys or semitones for this layer. On the [[Staff]] the number is visible at a glance: a third spans line-to-next-line or space-to-next-space.

**The quality: count semitones.** The number alone is too coarse. C–E and E–G are both thirds by letter count, but C–E spans four semitones and E–G only three. The four-semitone third is called **major** (M3), the three-semitone one **minor** (m3). Smaller quality = smaller by exactly one semitone, same letter distance.

Seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths all come in this major/minor pair. Unisons, fourths, fifths and octaves instead come in a single standard size called **perfect** — C–G (seven semitones) is a *perfect fifth*, C–F (five) a *perfect fourth*, and the octave (twelve) is perfect too. Shrink a perfect or minor interval by a semitone and it becomes **diminished**; stretch a perfect or major one and it becomes **augmented**. The only such interval the drills meet early is the [[Tritone]] — the odd distance between the perfect fourth and the perfect fifth.

The common intervals, in semitones:

| Interval | Semitones | Example |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| minor second (m2) | 1 | E–F |
| major second (M2) | 2 | C–D |
| minor third (m3) | 3 | E–G |
| major third (M3) | 4 | C–E |
| perfect fourth (P4) | 5 | C–F |
| tritone (A4/d5) | 6 | F–B |
| perfect fifth (P5) | 7 | C–G |
| octave (P8) | 12 | C–C |

Both layers matter because notation cares about spelling: C–E♭ and C–D♯ are the same number of semitones, but the first is a third (C–D–E: three letters) and the second a second (C–D: two). [[Enharmonic Equivalence]] makes them the same piano keys; interval naming keeps them distinct on purpose, because the spelling says which scale and chord the notes belong to.

Stacking intervals is where this pays off: a [[Third]] plus a third makes a [[Triad]], and which *kind* of third comes first decides the triad's quality. Read an interval upside down and you get its inversion — see [[Interval Inversion]] — and whether an interval sounds settled or tense is the subject of [[Consonance and Dissonance]].

## Common confusions

- **Counting keys instead of letters.** C–E is a third because it spans three *letters*, not because of any key count. Students who count semitones for the number get "C–E is a fifth" (five keys) — wrong layer.
- **Forgetting to count both endpoints.** C to E feels like "two steps up", but interval numbers count inclusively: C, D, E = 3.
- **"Perfect" is not praise.** A perfect fifth isn't better played or better sounding by definition — *perfect* is simply the quality name for the one-size intervals (unison, fourth, fifth, octave), as opposed to the major/minor pairs.
- **Same keys, different interval.** C–D♯ and C–E♭ use the same piano keys but are a second and a third respectively. Interval names live on the spelled letters, not the keyboard.
