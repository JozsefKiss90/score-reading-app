# Perfect Fifth

## Definition

The perfect fifth (P5) is the [[Interval]] spanning five letter names and seven semitones — C up to G. It is the most stable interval after the [[Octave]], with one standard size rather than a major/minor pair.

## Why it matters

The fifth is harmony's skeleton. Every major and minor [[Triad]] spans exactly a perfect fifth from root to top, so the fifth gives a chord its solidity while the third inside decides its colour. Between *keys*, the fifth is the unit of relatedness: the app transposes its drills around the [[Circle of Fifths]] one P5 at a time.

## Prerequisites

The [[Interval]] page's number/quality naming, and the [[Semitone]] for counting sizes.

## Explanation

Count the letters C, D, E, F, G — five, so C–G is a fifth. Count the semitones — seven — and it's a *perfect* fifth. Fifths belong to the perfect family: there is one standard size, not a major/minor pair.

Build a fifth on every note of the C-major scale and something remarkable happens: **six of the seven are perfect**. C–G, D–A, E–B, F–C, G–D, A–E all span seven semitones. The lone exception is B–F — only six semitones, a *diminished* fifth, better known as the [[Tritone]]. That one flaw in the pattern is where the scale's tension lives.

**Frame of the triad.** A major triad and a minor triad differ inside (M3+m3 vs m3+M3 — see [[Third]]) but both stack to the same seven-semitone frame: C–E–G and A–C–E each span a perfect fifth. Only the diminished triad (m3+m3, spanning the tritone) and the augmented triad (M3+M3, spanning eight semitones) break the frame — which is exactly why those two qualities sound unstable.

**Engine of the circle.** Step up a perfect fifth from C and you reach G, whose scale differs from C major by a single note. Keep stepping — D, A, E… — and you trace the entire circle of fifths: the fifth is the smallest move between keys, which is why neighbouring keys feel close.

## Common confusions

- **A fifth up ≠ a fifth apart in sound only.** C up a fifth is G, but G up a fifth is D — fifths chain into new territory rather than folding back like octaves. That chaining is the circle of fifths, not a repetition.
- **Not every scale fifth is perfect.** Students learn "fifths are perfect" and then miss B–F. Count B, C, D, E, F — a fifth by letters — but only six semitones: diminished. The exception matters more than the rule, because it powers dominant tension.
- **Fifth the interval vs. "the fifth" of a chord.** "A perfect fifth" is a distance; "the fifth of C major" is a chord member, the note G. The chord member is *named after* the interval it sits at.
