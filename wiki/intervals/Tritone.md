# Tritone

## Definition

The tritone is the [[Interval]] of six semitones — three whole tones, hence the name. It splits the [[Octave]] exactly in half, sits between the perfect fourth and the [[Perfect Fifth]], and is the most unstable interval the [[Major Scale]] contains.

## Why it matters

Stability needs a foil. The tritone is *the* dissonance that makes keys feel directional: the major scale contains exactly one, it demands resolution, and both notes of it resolve by semitone into the home chord. That pull is the engine of **dominant tension** — the drive that chords built on the scale's fifth note use to lead the music home — and everything the app later teaches about those dominant chords, sevenths, and applied chords runs on this one interval wanting to close.

## Prerequisites

The [[Interval]] naming layers, the [[Whole Tone]] (the tritone is three of them), and the [[Perfect Fifth]] it falls short of.

## Explanation

Six semitones can be spelled two ways, and the spelling decides the number:

- **F–B** — F, G, A, B is four letters: an *augmented fourth* (A4), a fourth stretched a semitone past perfect.
- **B–F** — B, C, D, E, F is five letters: a *diminished fifth* (d5), a fifth shrunk a semitone below perfect.

Same six semitones, same sound ([[Enharmonic Equivalence]] at the interval level); "tritone" is the umbrella name for both.

**The scale's one tritone.** Build every fourth and fifth in C major and all are perfect except the pair between F and B — the 4th and 7th degrees. That uniqueness is structural: hear a tritone and you know, within the key, exactly which two notes you're hearing and where they must go.

**How it resolves.** Each note lies a semitone from a note of the home chord: B pulls up to C, F pulls down to E. Spelled B–F (d5), the interval *contracts inward* to the third C–E; spelled F–B (A4), it *expands outward* to the sixth E–C. Play B–F then C–E at the piano and you hear the whole mechanism of dominant tension in two chords.

**Where it hides.** The diminished triad B–D–F is a tritone with a third filling it in — the reason that quality "wants to resolve". And the [[Dominant Seventh Chord]] carries the same B–F tritone inside G–B–D–F, which is why it pulls home so much harder than a bare triad. Later still, the [[Applied Dominant]] chords borrow a tritone from *outside* the key to point at a chord other than the tonic — the same engine, aimed elsewhere.

## In the Lab

The tritone first appears in the triad-quality drills as the diminished triad's outer frame: identify B–D–F as diminished and you have already spotted a tritone. The seventh-chord and applied-chord drills that build on it belong to later curriculum categories.

## Common confusions

- **A4 vs. d5 — same sound, different spelling, different resolution.** Students treat the two names as interchangeable. On the piano they are; on paper the spelling encodes the resolution (d5 contracts, A4 expands), which is why the drills care about spelling.
- **Tritone ≠ "three notes".** *Tri-* counts whole tones, not notes. It is an interval between two notes.
- **Not every dissonance is a tritone.** Seconds and sevenths are dissonant too (see [[Consonance and Dissonance]]); the tritone is special not for being harsh but for being the key's *unique*, direction-giving dissonance.
