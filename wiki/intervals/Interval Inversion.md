# Interval Inversion

## Definition

Inverting an [[Interval]] means moving its lower note up an octave (or the upper note down) so the two notes trade places. C–E, a major third, becomes E–C, a minor sixth. The notes are the same; the distance between them is measured from the other side.

## Why it matters

Inversion halves what you have to memorise: every interval and its flip are two views of one pair of notes, and the conversion follows two small rules. It is also the idea behind [[Chord Inversion]] — a triad with a note moved to the other side of the stack — so getting it right on two notes prepares the three-note version the drills grade.

## Prerequisites

The [[Interval]] naming layers and the [[Octave]] the flip happens around.

## Explanation

Take C–E and put the C up an octave: now E is on the bottom and the interval E–C spans E, F, G, A, B, C — a sixth. Two rules capture every case:

- **Numbers pair to nine.** A third inverts to a sixth (3+6=9), a second to a seventh, a fourth to a fifth, a unison to an octave. (Nine, not eight, because inclusive counting counts the shared note twice.)
- **Qualities swap — except perfect.** Major inverts to minor and back (C–E M3 → E–C m6); diminished inverts to augmented and back. *Perfect stays perfect*: C–G, a [[Perfect Fifth]], inverts to G–C, a perfect fourth. That self-symmetry is exactly why the one-size family is called perfect.

The semitone check: an interval and its inversion always sum to twelve, one octave (4+8, 7+5). The [[Tritone]] is the fixed point — six inverts to six, which is why its two spellings (A4 and d5) are each other's inversions.

Why this matters for chords: A–C–E and C–E–A contain identical pitches, but the second has the third-related pair C…A on the outside as a sixth. Recognising "a sixth is a flipped third" is what lets you see the same [[Triad]] in any vertical arrangement.

## In the Lab

No drill asks for interval inversion by name. It is baked into how the Lab grades instead: answers are checked by [[Pitch Class]], so any octave placement of the right notes is accepted — the drills let intervals invert freely under your hands, and the chord-inversion lessons in the *Chords* category make the flipped arrangements themselves the subject.

## Common confusions

- **Inversion ≠ going down.** E–C ascending and C–E descending are the *same* interval (a third, read downward). Inversion changes which note is on the bottom, not the direction you play in.
- **Numbers pair to nine, not eight.** "An octave has eight steps, so a third flips to a fifth" is the classic slip. Inclusive counting double-counts the pivot note: 3+6=9.
- **Interval inversion vs. chord inversion.** Related but distinct: interval inversion flips two notes around the octave; chord inversion re-bottoms a three-note stack. This page owns the two-note case; the chords domain owns the other.
