# Consonance and Dissonance

## Definition

Consonance and dissonance sort intervals by stability: a **consonant** interval sounds settled and could end a piece; a **dissonant** one sounds tense and asks to move somewhere. Harmony is the traffic between the two.

## Why it matters

Chord progressions work because dissonance creates motion and consonance grants rest. When the curriculum later says a chord "wants to resolve" — the diminished triad, the dominant seventh, a phrase landing home — it is always this interval-level fact underneath. Knowing which intervals carry the tension tells you *why* a progression moves, not just that it does.

## Prerequisites

The [[Interval]] naming layers; the [[Perfect Fifth]], [[Third]], and [[Tritone]] as the key examples.

## Explanation

The traditional sorting, at the depth the drills use it:

| Class | Intervals | Character |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| perfect consonances | unison, [[Octave]], perfect fifth (and usually the perfect fourth) | open, hollow, completely at rest |
| imperfect consonances | major & minor thirds and sixths | warm, full — the sound of triads |
| dissonances | major & minor seconds and sevenths, the tritone | tense, unresolved, in motion |

Play them in C major to calibrate your ear: C–G rings open; C–E is rich but stable; C–D grinds; B–F hangs in the air pointing somewhere. Note that quality and class cut differently — a *major* second is still a dissonance; a *minor* third is still a consonance. Stability follows the interval family, not the major/minor label.

**Dissonance is fuel, not error.** A piece of only consonances stands still. The dissonant intervals are what give music direction: a second wants to become a third, a seventh wants to sink, and the tritone — the scale's sharpest dissonance — resolves by semitones into the home chord's own notes. Composers spend dissonance to buy motion, then cash it in at points of arrival.

**From intervals to chords.** A chord inherits the character of its intervals. Major and minor triads contain only consonances (thirds, a perfect fifth) — that is why they can stand alone as endings. The diminished triad contains a tritone, so it cannot rest. This inheritance is the bridge from this page to everything in the harmony and cadence domains: *stable chord* and *unstable chord* are interval facts wearing chord names.

One footnote worth knowing: the perfect fourth is consonant between upper voices but was treated as a dissonance against the bass in older style. The drills never test this — file it as a curiosity until the voice-leading pages need it.

## In the Lab

The triad-quality drills are consonance training in disguise: *major* and *minor* answers name all-consonant chords, while *diminished* and *augmented* flag the unstable frames — students who listen for settled vs. tense classify faster than those who count semitones. The cadence and dominant-seventh categories later drill the resolution of dissonance directly.

## Common confusions

- **Dissonant ≠ wrong or ugly.** Dissonance is a functional role — the interval that carries motion. Music without it doesn't go anywhere.
- **"Minor" doesn't mean dissonant.** The minor third is fully consonant; the *major* second is dissonant. Consonance follows the interval family (thirds/sixths vs. seconds/sevenths), not the major/minor quality label.
- **Consonance is contextual at the edges.** The perfect fourth flips class depending on what's below it. The core table above is stable; the edges are style-dependent.
