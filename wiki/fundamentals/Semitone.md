# Semitone

## Definition

A semitone (half step) is the smallest distance between two notes on the piano: from any key to its immediate neighbour, black or white, with nothing in between.

## Why it matters

The semitone is music's unit of measurement. Scales, intervals, and chord qualities are all defined by counting semitones — get this distance in your ear and under your fingers, and every later definition in this vault has something to stand on.

## Prerequisites

The [[Note Names]], and [[Keyboard Geography]] — you need to see which keys are neighbours.

## Explanation

Start on C. The very next key upward is the black key C♯ (written with an [[Accidental]]): that step is one semitone. From E, though, the very next key is the *white* key F — E and F touch directly, so E–F is also a semitone. The same happens at B–C.

Count the neighbouring steps inside one [[Octave]] and you get **twelve semitones** — the twelve keys, white and black together, before the pattern repeats.

Two semitones make a [[Whole Tone]], and the alternating pattern of tones and semitones is exactly what gives the [[Major Scale]] its shape.

## In the Lab

The *Scales* lessons lean on the whole/half-step pattern in every key, and the harmonic-minor drills hinge on a single semitone: one note is raised to sit just a semitone below the key's home note, and hearing that pull is the point of the exercise.

## Common confusions

- **A semitone is not "white to black".** E–F and B–C are white-to-white semitones; conversely C–D is white-to-white but *two* semitones. Count neighbours, not colours.
- **A semitone is a distance, not a note.** "C♯" names a key; "a semitone" names how far it is from C. Students conflate the step with the note that ends it.
