# Whole Tone

## Definition

A whole tone (whole step) is a distance of two semitones — from a key to its neighbour's neighbour, with exactly one key skipped in between.

## Why it matters

Scales are built from just two step sizes, and this is the bigger one. Once you can tell a whole tone from a semitone by eye and by ear, scale patterns like W W H W W W H stop being formulas and become something you can check at the keyboard.

## Prerequisites

The [[Semitone]] — a whole tone is two of them.

## Explanation

From C, skip C♯ and land on D: C–D is a whole tone. From E, skip F and land on F♯: also a whole tone — the skipped key can be black or white, what matters is that exactly one key lies between.

The [[Major Scale]] is the classic arrangement: **whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half** (W W H W W W H). In C major that spells C–D–E–F–G–A–B–C, with the two half steps falling naturally at E–F and B–C. Started anywhere other than C, the same pattern needs an [[Accidental]] or several — but that construction story belongs to the major-scale page.

## In the Lab

The *Scales* lessons drill exactly this pattern: block and arpeggiated scale-degree work in every major key, where each new key re-lays the same whole/half template onto different keys of the instrument.

## Common confusions

- **White neighbours aren't automatically a whole tone.** C–D is a whole tone, but E–F and B–C are semitones. The keyboard's geography, not the note letters, decides the size.
- **Whole tone vs. whole note.** A whole *tone* is a pitch distance; a whole *note* is a duration (a note lasting a full measure). The words are close; the ideas are unrelated.

## Related Concepts

The [[Semitone]] is the other scale step; the [[Major Scale]] arranges the two into the drills' favourite pattern; an [[Interval]] generalises "distance between notes" far beyond these two sizes.
