# Accidental

## Definition

An accidental is a sign written before a note that shifts its pitch: a sharp (♯) raises it one semitone, a flat (♭) lowers it one semitone, and a natural (♮) cancels a previous sharp or flat.

## Why it matters

The seven letter names only cover the white keys. Accidentals unlock the other five notes per octave — and in this app they also carry meaning: when a drill shows you a ♯ or ♮ mid-phrase, it is usually pointing at the one note that makes the harmony work.

## Prerequisites

[[Note Names]], the [[Semitone]] (the distance every accidental moves), and [[Keyboard Geography]].

## Explanation

Take C: add a sharp and you get **C♯**, the black key one semitone up. Take B: add a flat and you get **B♭**, the black key one semitone down. Most sharps and flats land on black keys, but not all — raise E by a semitone and you land on the white key F, so spellings like E♯ exist (the drills keep to practical spellings, but the principle matters for [[Enharmonic Equivalence]]).

Two reading rules:

- An accidental is written *before* the note head on the [[Staff]], but spoken *after* the letter: you see ♯ then the note, you say "C sharp".
- An accidental applies to that note **for the rest of its measure** (see [[Beat and Measure]]), then expires at the barline. The ♮ sign exists to cancel one early.

A key can also declare accidentals up front for the whole piece — that standing declaration is a [[Key Signature]], and telling the two apart is a classic stumbling block (see below).

## In the Lab

The harmonic-minor lessons are built around exactly one accidental: in A minor the seventh degree G is raised to G♯, and the drills render that ♯ right on the score rather than hiding it in a signature — the accidental *is* the lesson, marking the one raised note the harmony hinges on.

## Common confusions

- **Accidental vs. key signature.** An accidental is local (this note, this measure); a [[Key Signature]] is global (this letter, the whole piece). Same symbols, very different reach.
- **It lasts to the barline, not for one note.** After a C♯, every later C in that measure and octave is also sharp unless a ♮ intervenes.
- **Sharp of one note can be flat of another.** C♯ and D♭ are the same key on the instrument — [[Enharmonic Equivalence]] covers when each spelling is right.
