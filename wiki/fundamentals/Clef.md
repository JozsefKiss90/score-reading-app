# Clef

## Definition

A clef is the symbol at the left end of a staff that fixes which pitch each line and space means. This app uses two: the treble clef for higher notes and the bass clef for lower ones.

## Why it matters

Without a clef, a staff is just five anonymous lines. With one, every position has a name — and the piano needs both clefs at once, because its range is too wide for either alone.

## Prerequisites

The [[Staff]] and the [[Note Names]].

## Explanation

**Treble clef** (𝄞) — also called the G clef: its inner curl wraps around the second line from the bottom, branding it **G** (the G above middle C). From that anchor the lines read E–G–B–D–F going up, and the spaces spell F–A–C–E.

**Bass clef** (𝄢) — the F clef: its two dots straddle the fourth line, branding it **F** (the F below middle C). Its lines read G–B–D–F–A, its spaces A–C–E–G.

The anchors are the part worth memorising; every other position is reachable by counting letters up or down from them. The two windows meet around middle C — the [[Ledger Line]] page shows exactly where it sits, and the [[Grand Staff]] is built on that hinge.

## In the Lab

The drills put your right hand on the treble staff and your left on the bass staff. Chord drills often split a harmony across both clefs — bass note below, upper voices above — so both anchor points need to be reflexive.

## Common confusions

- **Same position, different clef, different note.** The bottom line is E in treble but G in bass. Students who learn one clef first often "read treble" on the bass staff — the most common misreading in early grand-staff work.
- **The clefs overlap; they don't stack end-to-end.** The treble staff's low notes and the bass staff's high notes share the region around middle C — the clefs are two windows onto one pitch continuum, not two separate floors.
