# Keyboard Geography

## Definition

Keyboard geography is knowing the piano's repeating pattern of white and black keys well enough to find any named note without hesitation — starting with finding C.

## Why it matters

The app listens to a MIDI keyboard: every answer you give is a key you press. If finding an E♭ takes searching, no amount of theory helps — the drills assume your hands know the map.

## Prerequisites

The [[Note Names]] — the seven letters the white keys carry.

## Explanation

Look at the black keys: they come in alternating **groups of two and three**, and this two-three pattern repeats along the whole keyboard. The pattern is your landmark system:

- **C** is the white key immediately to the *left of any two-black-key group*.
- **F** is the white key immediately to the left of any three-black-key group.

From any C, the white keys ascend C D E F G A B, then the next C — one full [[Octave]], after which everything repeats. Middle C is the C nearest the middle of the keyboard (a keyboard label or your instrument's manual will confirm which one on smaller keyboards).

The black keys take their names from their white neighbours with a sharp or flat — the details belong to [[Accidental]].

## In the Lab

The technique warm-up drills start in five-finger C position — thumb on C, one finger per white key — precisely so the geography becomes automatic before harder material arrives. Every other drill then relies on it silently: the prompt names notes, your hands must find them.

## Common confusions

- **Not every pair of white neighbours has a black key between them.** E–F and B–C touch directly — the gap between them is only a [[Semitone]], the same distance as white-to-black elsewhere.
- **The pattern, not the position, identifies the note.** Students sometimes learn "C is *here*" on one keyboard and get lost on another. Anchor on the two-black-key group, and any keyboard reads the same.

## Related Concepts
