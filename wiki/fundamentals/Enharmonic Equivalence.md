# Enharmonic Equivalence

## Definition

Two notes are enharmonically equivalent when they are spelled differently but played on the same key — F♯ and G♭ are two names for one sound.

## Why it matters

Sooner or later a drill in a flat key will show you a note you learned under a sharp name. Knowing that the *sound and the key under your finger* are identical — and that only the spelling changed — turns a moment of panic into a shrug.

## Prerequisites

[[Accidental]] (sharps and flats create the double names) and [[Keyboard Geography]].

## Explanation

Every black key sits between two white neighbours, so every black key answers to two everyday names: the sharp of the key below and the flat of the key above. C♯ = D♭, D♯ = E♭, F♯ = G♭, G♯ = A♭, A♯ = B♭.

Which name is *right* depends on context. A scale must use each letter A–G exactly once, so G major spells its seventh note **F♯** (the letters run G A B C D E F♯) — calling it G♭ would use G twice and skip F. The spelling encodes the note's role; the sound doesn't change.

White keys have enharmonic spellings too (E♯ = F, C♭ = B), though the drills stick to practical spellings and you will mostly meet the black-key pairs.

## In the Lab

The curriculum's *Circle of Fifths* entry opens the circle itself, and travelling it clockwise through the sharp keys lands you where the flat keys begin — the same keys on the instrument reappearing under flat names (F♯ territory respelled as G♭). And the grader is on your side here: it listens to which key you press, not how the score spells it, so enharmonic spelling never costs you a correct answer — see [[Pitch Class]].

## In the Atlas

The Circle of Fifths view makes the seam visible: travel clockwise through the sharps and you arrive where the flat keys begin, at the F♯/G♭ joint of the wheel.

## Common confusions

- **Same sound ≠ same note on paper.** F♯ and G♭ are interchangeable to the ear but not to notation — in G major, writing G♭ is simply wrong spelling, even though it plays fine.
- **Enharmonic pairs are not "sharp = flat" in general.** C♯ equals D♭, not C♭. Each pairing straddles one specific key; students sometimes over-generalise the ♯/♭ swap.
