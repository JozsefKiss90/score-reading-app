# Grand Staff

## Definition

The grand staff is two staves joined by a brace and read as one: a treble staff on top for the right hand, a bass staff below for the left. It is how piano music — and every score in this app — is written.

## Why it matters

Grand-staff reading appears in essentially every drill the Lab offers. Whatever a drill asks — a scale to play, a chord to name, a phrase to hear — the question arrives as a grand staff and your answer leaves through the keyboard. It is the single most-exercised skill in the app.

## Prerequisites

Both [[Clef|clefs]] and [[Ledger Line|ledger lines]] — the grand staff is those two ideas joined at middle C.

## Explanation

Picture the two staves as windows onto one tall ladder of pitches. The bass staff shows the lower stretch, the treble staff the upper, and they meet in the middle at **middle C** — one piano key, reachable from both windows on a ledger line (exactly where it sits is the ledger-line page's story).

Reading conventions the drills follow:

- Top staff, treble clef → usually the **right hand**; bottom staff, bass clef → the **left hand**.
- The vertical gap between the staves is typographic breathing room, not missing notes — only a few pitches (around middle C) live there, on ledger lines.
- Bar lines run through both staves together: the two hands share one timeline (see [[Beat and Measure]]).

A chord written across both staves — bass note low, upper notes high — is *one* harmony split between the hands, the layout the Lab's chord drills use constantly.

## In the Lab

Everything renders here: there is no category that doesn't put a grand staff in front of you — the drills differ in *what* they ask, never in *where* you read it.

## Common confusions

- **It's one instrument's view, not two.** Students sometimes treat the staves as independent pieces of music. The brace means: same music, same timeline, split by register and hand.
- **The hands can trespass.** "Treble = right hand" is a default, not a law — a low melody may put the right hand on the bass staff. The clef tells you the pitch; the context tells you the hand.
