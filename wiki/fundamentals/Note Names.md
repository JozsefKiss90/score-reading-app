# Note Names

## Definition

Music names its pitches with just seven letters — A, B, C, D, E, F, G — used over and over. After G the alphabet starts again at A, one octave higher.

## Why it matters

Every scale, chord, and drill in this app is spelled with these seven letters. Before you can say what a C major triad is, you need to know what "C" means and why the next C up the keyboard has the same name.

## Prerequisites

None — this is where the vault starts. Everything else builds on it.

## Explanation

The seven letters repeat in a fixed loop:

> … A B C D E F G **A** B C D E F G **A** …

Each letter names one white key per cycle on the piano, and adjacent letters sit on adjacent white keys — B is the white key just above A, C just above B. When the loop wraps from G back to A, you have climbed one [[Octave]]: the new A sounds like a higher copy of the old one, which is exactly why it earns the same name.

Where each letter physically lives on the keyboard is its own small skill — see [[Keyboard Geography]]. The letters can also be raised or lowered by a [[Accidental|sharp or flat]], which is how the remaining five notes per octave get their names.

## In the Lab

The *Scales* category asks you to play chords on named scale degrees across many keys — the prompts and feedback speak in these letter names (C major, E♭ major, F♯), so fluent letter-reading is assumed from the first drill onwards.

## In the Atlas

The Global Diatonic Map lists each key's seven notes by letter name, one row per key — a quick place to see the alphabet cycling through every key.

## Common confusions

- **After G comes A, not H.** The loop is seven letters long; there is no eighth letter (in this app's English convention).
- **Same letter, different height.** Every A on the piano shares the name but not the pitch. The name tells you the *family* a note belongs to — the octave tells you which member. [[Pitch Class]] makes this idea precise.
